You did three tours in Iraq with the CIA. What lessons did you learn about American power and what it is capable of and what it can't achieve? The most important thing I think I learned from my time, certainly in the 20 years after 9-11, was you better understand what you're doing before you take military action, and it better be limited and discreet, because the American public, unless we are, again, facing truly imminent threats that everyone can understand, they're not looking for long, expensive wars abroad. We haven't proven ourselves very good at them, and the American public just doesn't want to be staying in there for these long periods of time. So for me, certainly, anyone, I think, who served in Iraq or Afghanistan is certainly chastened a bit on what we can do. And then, I think, certainly, we're in a new era in terms of technology and the future of warfare. And while we were bogged down in Iraq and Afghanistan, folks like the Chinese military were just sitting there, thinking through all the ways that they could win the next era. And it is very clear to me that while we were investing in land vehicles and armor and protecting ourselves from IEDs, they were thinking about satellites and space and how to make us go blind, deaf, and dumb in the first moves of a conflict. I think there is an opportunity cost to everything you do, and I think those wars taught me, at least, that while you're looking over here, your adversaries, your competitors, are thinking about ways to undercut you over here.
