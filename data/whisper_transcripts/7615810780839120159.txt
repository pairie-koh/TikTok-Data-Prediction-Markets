Let's build a machine learning model to predict the price of your favorite stock together. If I had to describe this project in one sentence, I'd say we're building a model that looks at patterns in past stock behavior and in the broader market and then uses those patterns to make an educated guess about how the stock might perform over the next few trading days. So let's do exactly this for my favorite stock, which is NVIDIA. A really important part of the methodology is deciding what exactly we want the model to predict. At first, it might seem like the goal should be to predict NVIDIA's exact future stock price, but that's actually not the best way to approach the problem. Instead, we predict something called a forward return. In this case, the forward return is the percent change in NVIDIA's stock price over the next five days. This is a much better target because stock prices themselves can be messy. A return is more useful because it tells us where the stock has moved relative to where it started. Once we know what we are predicting, the next step is gathering data. Clearly, we need examples from the past so the model has something to learn from. So we're pulling NVIDIA's historical stock data, but we don't stop there because NVIDIA does not move in isolation. Stock is influenced not only by company-specific news, but also by what is happening in the overall market, in the technology sector, and especially in the semiconductor industry. And that is why we're also including data from the S&P 500, from the tech-heavy Nasdaq, from the semiconductor industry, and from the VIX. After collecting the data, we move on to one of the most important steps in the process, which is feature engineering. Features are simply pieces of information that we give the model so that it can make its prediction to the best of its ability. For example, we might give it things like recent daily returns, measures of volatility, moving averages, changes in trading volume, and the difference between NVIDIA's performance and the performance of the broader market. Some of these features sound super technical, but the ideas behind them are actually really intuitive. A moving average, for instance, is just the average stock price over a certain period, such as the last 5 or 20 trading days. This helps us understand whether the stock is trading above or below its recent trend. Volatility, on the other hand, tells us how wildly the stock has been moving. And the volume of the trades matters as well, because unusually heavy trading can signal more conviction behind a single move. At this point, we have both our inputs and our outputs. The inputs are the features, which describe what the market looked like on any given day. And the output is the target, which tells us what actually happened to the NVIDIA stock over the next 5 trading days. And this is what makes our model a supervised machine learning problem. For each day in the dataset, we show the model clues that were available at the time of prediction, and we also show the future outcome that followed. This brings us to my favorite part of the pipeline, training. Training is the process where the model repeatedly looks at historical data, makes a prediction, compares that prediction to what actually happened, and then adjusts itself so it can do better next time. And over time, it gets better at connecting certain combinations of features to certain outcomes, just like a human. In our case, we are using a model called XGBoost. It's especially good for structured numerical data like we have here. It works by building many small decision trees and combining them in a smart way, so that together they can capture more complex patterns than a simple linear model would be able to. However, building the model is only half the job. The other half is making sure we test it honestly. This is one of the biggest places where beginner stock models go wrong. When we have time-based data like we do here, we can never randomly shuffle the data to split it into training and testing sets, because that would let the model learn from the future and then pretend it predicted the past. Instead, we always use a time-based split. Essentially, we train the model on older data and test it on newer data it's never seen before. And this step is absolutely crucial when it comes to preventing data leakage. To evaluate, we compare the model's predicted five-day returns to the actual five-day return. And there are a few ways to measure this performance. Evaluation metrics like mean absolute error and root mean squared error tell us how far off the model's predictions were on average. Direction accuracy tells us how often the model got at least the direction of the move correctly. After evaluation, we often do something called a back test. A back test takes the model's predictions and asks a more practical question. If we had actually used these predictions to make real decisions, what would have happened? For example, we might create a simple rule that says that we only buy the NVIDIA stock if the model predicts that it will rise by more than 2% over the next five trading days. Then we apply this rule to the historical test data and see how the strategy would have performed. This gives the model the ability to simulate actual trading decisions and investment outcomes even though its core prediction is the stock price. Now let's take a look at the outcome of this back test. Our model generated 231 buy signals, won about 58% of those trades with an average gain of 0.61% per trade, and would have produced a cumulative return of roughly 190% over the test period.
