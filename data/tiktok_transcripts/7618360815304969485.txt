This reporter says gamblers threatened to kill him unless he changed his story. Let me explain. On March 10th, Times of Israel reporter Emanuel Fabian reported about an Iranian missile that hit right outside of the Israeli city of Bet Shemesh. Fabian posted video of the attack to social media and reported that no one was injured. But then something unusual happened. Messages started pouring in, emails asking for a correction saying that the missile didn't actually hit, even though he had video evidence proving otherwise. He said in the times of Israel that he was confused as to why people were focused on such a seemingly minor detail. Now he believes the messages he received were to confirm whether a missile hit Israel to settle a prediction on polymarket. Since missiles or drones that are intercepted don't count as a hit. Fabian thinks internet gamblers banking against a hit were the ones targeting him and pressuring him to change his reporting. When he refused to change his reporting, that's when he said the threats started. Multiple messages on WhatsApp by someone called Chaim you have exactly half an hour to correct your attempted influence. You made a fatal mistake and you'd better respond to us. You will pay the full price for your irresponsible act. Polymarket issued a statement condemning the harassment against him, but a bet on the platform for a March 10th strike garnered 22 million in trading volume. Fabian told the times of Israel that the threats have stopped But it's a glimpse into the dark side of prediction markets. And it also highlights a new problem reporters are facing when the news itself becomes something that people try to manipulate.
